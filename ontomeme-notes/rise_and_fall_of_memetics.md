# Rise and Fall of Memetics

After the initial burst of creativity in the 1980s, Memetics entered a period of intense development — then stalled.

## The 1990s: The Golden Age

### 1985–1986: New frameworks

- **Douglas Hofstadter** (1985): "Metamagical Themes" — provided an "anatomical breakdown" of Memetics, introducing:
  - **Schemes**: patterns of thought and behavior
  - **Co-memes**: related memes that reinforce each other
  - **Bait and hooks**: the mechanisms that make memes sticky and attractive

### 1991: The Virus Analogy

- **Richard Dawkins**: "The Virus of the Mind" — focused on religious beliefs and activities
- Differentiated "religious memes" from computer viruses and scientific findings
- The metaphor of memes as "viruses of the mind" became iconic

### 1992–2008: The Heylighen Project

Francis Heylighen began his work on Memetics and produced a sustained body of articles:
- 1992, 1996, 1998a, 1998b, 2005, 2008

His most significant contributions:
- Theory on different **types of meme replication and adaptation**
- Emphasis on the need for **suitable memetic units** for Memetics to be tested empirically
- Laid groundwork for computational models of memetic evolution

## The Conceptual Boom

### 1993: Neuroscience Meets Memetics

- **Daniel Dennett**: "Consciousness Explained" — integrated memes with experimental neuroscience and cognitive psychology
- This was a major step: memes weren't just cultural theory anymore, they were connected to how brains actually work

### 1996: Darwin's Dangerous Idea

Dennett's later work extended his understanding of memes, pointing out different **challenges in operationalizing** the concept. This was an honest reckoning: the theory was good, but making it testable was hard.

### 1996: Thought Contagion

- **Ariel Lynch**: "Thought Contagion: How Belief Spreads Through Society"
- Used **everyday life examples** to develop the conceptual basis
- Made memetics accessible: ideas spread like emotions through gossip, conversation, shared experience

## The Institutional Push

### 1997: Journal of Memetics

The online *Journal of Memetics – Evolutionary Models of Information Transmission* was created. Initial optimism was high — a dedicated venue for the new discipline.

### 1999: The Meme Machine

- **Susan Blackmore** (psychologist): "The Meme Machine"
- Attempted to **constitute Memetics as a science**
- Debated the **empirical and analytic potential** of the emerging discipline
- One of the most popular science books on the topic

### 2000: Darwinizing Culture

Edited by **Robert Aunger**, with contributions from both proponents and critics — a snapshot of a field at its peak, unsure of itself.

### 2002: The Electric Meme

Aunger's book focused on memes **as objects in brains** — shifting from the cultural to the neural.

## The Decline

Then it became silent. From most of the pioneers.

Why?

- The **Journal of Memetics** did not become the intended arena for progressive growth
- Its **center of gravity was weak** — no unifying framework held it together
- **Contributions dropped** steadily
- The journal was **closed down in 2005**

## What happened?

Three possible reasons:

1. **No empirical tests**: Without measurable units (as Heylighen warned), Memetics couldn't produce the kind of reproducible results that convince science
2. **Too controversial**: The "sociobiology wars" poisoned the well — many academics dismissed it by association
3. **The internet changed things**: As online communities grew, meme-like phenomena became visible but escaped the label

The field fractured into:
- **Cognitive science** (neural models of cultural transmission)
- **Evolutionary psychology** (how brains encode cultural patterns)
- **Internet culture studies** (memes as jokes and viral content)

The original unified theory — Memetics as a discipline — disappeared into pieces.
