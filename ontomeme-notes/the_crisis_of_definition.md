# The Crisis of Definition

Robert Aunger's 2002 claim — **"No one knows what a meme is"** — became the defining indictment of Memetics.

But the problem went deeper than semantics.

## The Operational Problem

Aunger's point was this: if the **ontological units of analysis are missing**, Memetics cannot function as a science.

- Without a defined unit, you can't **measure** it
- Without measurement, you can't **test** it
- Without testing, it's not science — it's speculation

The externalist/internalist debate was **hypothetical and counter-productive**. While scholars argued about *where memes live*, the field had no **operational definition** of *what a meme is* — the kind of definition needed for empirical research.

## The Consensus Criticism

Three researchers independently arrived at the same conclusion:

### Edmonds (2002)
Memetics' theoretical statements are **too vague to be empirically tested**. No testable hypotheses, no falsifiable predictions.

### Gil-Field (2002)
Memetics suffers from **conceptual confusion** and a lack of empirical work. Lots of talk, no data.

### Aunger (2000)
Memetics should follow the progress of **evolutionary cultural studies more generally**, as practiced in anthropology. The field was moving in other disciplines — Memetics was inventing wheels.

**The clock was ticking.**

## The Responses

Scholars recognized the crisis. Three distinct responses emerged:

### 1. Chielens & Heylighen (2005): Four Stages

They introduced a structural solution:

**Four stages of memetic replication:**
1. **Transmission** — the meme is communicated
2. **Internalization** — it enters a recipient's memory
3. **Retention** — it is stored in the nervous system
4. **Externalization** — it is reproduced in behavior/artifact

**Four ways memes adapt:**
- Modification during transmission
- Integration with existing knowledge
- Competition with similar memes
- Mutation through reinterpretation

They argued for a **suitable memetic unit** for empirical testing. Examples:
- **Storylines** — different elements of stories as individual memes
- **Digital virus hoaxes** — email messages warning about non-existing viruses

### 2. Aunger (2002): Wait for Neuroscience

Aunger didn't abandon the project — he deferred it.

His hope: **neuroscience will measure the electric difference** between memes when fired as diverse impulses between synapses in the brain.

> *"When you think of the meme 'taco' or 'Killroy was here', different electric impulses fire, which can be distinct and determined, and can be differentiated, defined and measured."*

The problem: **technology wasn't ready.** We didn't yet have the tools to measure "the electric difference" between a taco meme and a Killroy meme in a living brain.

### 3. Hull (2001): Postpone and Start

**Daniel Hull** took a pragmatic approach:

> *"Postpone definitional concerns. Start empirical research."*

His prescription:

1. **Develop different memetic theories** influenced by the domains in which they're tested (anthropology, psychology, communication)
2. **Distinguish between the meme and its phenotype** — the observable outward manifestation
3. **Investigate the observable attributes** (the phenotype), not the underlying meme itself
4. **Have a clear goal** every time memetic research is conducted

In biology, this is the **genotype vs phenotype** distinction. The gene (genotype) is invisible. What we measure are the traits it produces (phenotype). Hull said Memetics should do the same: study the **observable effects** of memes, not the memes themselves.

## The Stakes

If Hull is right, Memetics can survive by studying **what memes do** — their effects on culture, behavior, communication — without needing to define what they *are*.

If Aunger is right, Memetics is waiting for neuroscience to catch up.

If both are wrong, the field may be terminally vague — a metaphor with nothing behind it.
